# Cascade/Ensemble Classifier Pipelines for Gravitational Lens Finding and Rare-Class Detection

## Has anyone built a cascade/multi-stage pipeline specifically for gravitational lens finding with a stage-2 specialist for compound lenses, rings, or other rare configurations?

### Takeaway
No paper in the literature surveyed describes a lens-finding pipeline with an explicit **stage-1 generalist → stage-2 rare-configuration specialist classifier** architecture (i.e., a dedicated second-stage model trained specifically to re-classify or rescue compound lenses/double rings that a general lens-finder flags as ambiguous or low-confidence). Wilde et al. (2022) is the key paper establishing *that* CNNs are differentially worse at rare configurations, but it does not propose or test a cascade/specialist fix — it ends with a general recommendation that "attention must be paid to training the lens finding algorithms to accommodate the anticipated interesting subsets," with no concrete architecture given. Existing "two-stage" or "multi-stage" lens-finding pipelines in the literature (HOLISMOKES-II, AgileLens, Euclid Strong Lensing Discovery Engine) are stage-wise by *computational cost/data volume* (cheap catalog-level cut → expensive image-level CNN → human/citizen inspection), not by *rarity of configuration type*. This is a genuine gap: the specific "generalist-then-compound-lens-specialist" architecture the user is contemplating does not yet exist in print.

### Cited Findings
- Wilde, Serjeant, Bromley, Dickinson, Koopmans & Metcalf, "Detecting gravitational lenses using machine learning: exploring interpretability and sensitivity to rare lensing configurations," MNRAS 512, 3464–3479 (2022), arXiv:2202.12776 — CNNs (OU-200, OU-66, trained on Euclid-like VIS/Y/J/H simulations) achieve F1 = 0.83–0.91 overall but recall drops sharply for rare configurations: OU-200 recall = 0.7505 (compound arcs) and 0.5567 (double rings) vs. much higher recall for ordinary single lenses on the same architecture (Table 5/6 of the paper); OU-66 recall = 0.6291 (arcs) / 0.4742 (double rings). — [arXiv:2202.12776](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2202.12776)
- The same paper tests an ensemble/fusion approach, "OU-T-SNE," which combines seven independently-trained per-band/band-combination models via t-SNE dimensionality reduction of their output vectors, classified on a 100×100 embedded grid — but this ensemble *underperforms* the single best model OU-200 (F1 = 0.9423 for OU-T-SNE vs. 0.9687 for OU-200 alone), i.e., naive ensembling of generalist models did not help, let alone specifically rescue rare classes. — [arXiv:2202.12776](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2202.12776)
- The paper's own stated future-work framing is generic, not architectural: "it is less clear that these red lensed systems will be detectable by the lens finder presented in this paper," with human visual inspection proposed as the fallback/complement for unknown exotic systems rather than a learned second-stage specialist. — [arXiv:2202.12776](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2202.12776)
- HOLISMOKES-II (Pan-STARRS; arXiv:2004.13048) uses a two-step pipeline: a cheap catalog-level neural network (using multi-band photometry/colors) first reduces an all-sky input catalog down to order ~1 million candidate sources, and only then is a second, more expensive image-level CNN (trained on Pan-STARRS gri cutouts) run to produce ranked candidate lists for human vetting. This split is explicitly for *computational/triage efficiency over a huge input catalog*, not for routing different lens morphologies to different specialist models. — [arXiv:2004.13048](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.13048)
- AgileLens (Euclid Q1; arXiv:2604.06648, published in A&A 712, A207) is described as "an end-to-end, iterative pipeline" where a VIS-only "seed classifier" supplies clear positives/typical impostors from a small initial seed set (27 lenses, augmented ×67 to 1,809, plus 2,000 negatives), used to build a morphology-balanced training set; a modified VGG16 (GlobalAveragePooling + 256/128 dense head, last 9 layers trainable) is then iteratively fine-tuned over 3 rounds on a growing 30,686-image VIS+NISP color dataset. This is a *self-training/iterative-refinement* cascade (same generalist model re-trained on its own confident outputs), not a generalist/specialist split by lens type. Final yield: 441 Grade A/B candidates from human grading of the top 4,000 ranked images, including 130 new candidates not in the existing Q1 lens catalogue. — [arXiv:2604.06648](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.06648)
- The Euclid Q1 "Strong Lensing Discovery Engine" (arXiv:2503.15328) ensembles **8 independently-trained ML networks from 5 different teams** plus citizen-science visual inspection in a tiered workflow: (1) 8 networks score ~1 million images; (2) citizen scientists review ~100,000 images (65% chosen via high network scores, 35% random for calibration); (3) expert graders assign confidence grades A–D; (4) outputs are combined into a final ensemble classification. Reported performance: purity 52±2% and completeness 50% for combined grade A/B lenses, against a baseline random-classifier purity of only 0.05% given the intrinsic rarity of lenses. The paper does not report results broken out by lens morphology (compound vs. single), so it is unclear whether ensembling specifically helps rare configurations more than common ones. — [arXiv:2503.15328](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.15328)
- González, Collett, Rojas, Bechtol et al., "Does Machine Learning Work? A Comparative Analysis of Strong Gravitational Lens Searches in the Dark Energy Survey" (arXiv:2510.23782) independently runs three separate ML lens searches over the same DES Year-6 imaging (reducing ~236 million cutouts to 22,564 targets using a pre-trained Vision Transformer among other models) and reports that **combining the three independent searches improves completeness and reduces missed discoveries** relative to any single search — functionally an ensemble-of-independent-generalists approach rather than a generalist+specialist cascade, but directly relevant evidence that multiple independent models catch different lenses than any one model alone. — [arXiv:2510.23782](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.23782)
- "The Strong Gravitational Lens Finding Challenge" (Metcalf, Meneghetti, Avestruz et al., A&A 625, A119, 2019), arXiv:1802.03609 — notes that "the most difficult challenge for a lens finder is differentiating between rare, irregular and ring-like face-on galaxies and true gravitational lenses," i.e., independently confirms (pre-dating Wilde et al.) that ring-like/rare configurations are the hard tail for ML lens finders, but the challenge paper evaluates individual competing methods rather than proposing a cascade architecture. — [arXiv:1802.03609](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1802.03609)
- AnomalyMatch (arXiv:2505.03509/2505.03508), a semi-supervised + active-learning method framing rare-object discovery as extreme-imbalance binary classification, has been applied to find 57 gravitational lenses among 600,000 JWST sources and 61 "jellyfish" galaxies among 380,000 Euclid Q1 sources, learning from "minimal labelled anomaly examples." This is the closest analog found to a "specialist trained on very few rare-class examples" approach being applied to lens discovery, but it is a single-stage semi-supervised classifier for anomalies generally, not a stage-2 specialist conditioned on a stage-1 generalist's output, and it is not focused specifically on compound-lens vs. single-lens discrimination. — [arXiv:2505.03509](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.03509)

### Inferences
- The lens-finding literature has clearly identified the *problem* (rare configurations are harder — Wilde et al. 2022, Metcalf et al. 2019) and has built *ensemble-for-coverage* and *cost-cascaded* pipelines, but no team has yet closed the loop with a *configuration-conditioned specialist cascade*. This appears to be an open methodological contribution, not a previously-tried-and-abandoned idea — the Wilde et al. ensemble attempt (OU-T-SNE) that underperformed was a same-task ensemble, not a specialist-routing cascade, so it does not constitute evidence against the specific architecture being considered.
- The existing two-stage pipelines (HOLISMOKES-II, AgileLens) establish that the *computational* pattern — cheap/broad filter followed by an expensive/narrow classifier — is well-proven and successful in this exact subfield, which is encouraging precedent for the general cascade *concept*, even though none of them split by rarity/morphology.

### Gaps
- No ablation exists comparing a single generalist end-to-end classifier against a generalist+specialist cascade specifically on compound lenses/double rings — this experiment appears not to have been run or published yet in the lens-finding literature as of early/mid-2026.
- No paper reports how a stage-1 lens/non-lens classifier's false negatives on compound lenses would interact with a stage-2 specialist (i.e., whether the specialist could ever see configurations the generalist filters out) — this is addressed only generically in the non-lens cascade-critique literature (see final section below).

## In astronomical object detection/classification more broadly, what examples exist of cascade or multi-stage ML pipelines, and what benefit do they report over a single end-to-end classifier?

### Takeaway
Multi-stage pipelines are common in astronomy, but almost always split by **cost/volume** (coarse-cheap filter → fine-expensive classifier) or by **taxonomy depth** (coarse class → fine subclass), rather than by routing hard/rare cases to a specialist; reported benefits are typically in compute efficiency and in purity/completeness trade-offs rather than head-to-head "cascade beats single-stage" ablations with matched compute.

### Cited Findings
- HOLISMOKES-II (arXiv:2004.13048): catalog-level network (cheap, photometry/colors) + image-level CNN (expensive, pixel data) — explicitly reduces an all-sky Pan-STARRS catalog to ~1 million candidates before the costlier CNN stage runs, which the authors state is "efficient in selecting galaxy-scale strong lenses from very wide-field surveys without imposing restrictive cuts on the input galaxy catalog." — [arXiv:2004.13048](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.13048)
- ORACLE (Shah et al., arXiv:2501.01496) is "the first hierarchical deep-learning model for real-time, context-aware classification" of LSST transients/variables: a recurrent GRU-based network trained with a custom hierarchical cross-entropy loss that outputs classifications along an observationally-driven taxonomy, with confidence increasing as more photometric epochs arrive; the hierarchy is explicitly motivated to help "earlier identification...of rare transients such as tidal disruption events (TDEs) and kilonovae." This is architecturally a *single* network with a hierarchy-aware loss rather than literally cascaded separate models, but it targets exactly the same goal (earlier/better detection of the rare tail) via hierarchical structure instead of independent stage-2 specialists. — [arXiv:2501.01496](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.01496)
- NEEDLE (NEural Engine for Discovering Luminous Events; arXiv:2312.04968, MNRAS 531, 2474) is a hybrid CNN+dense-network classifier built specifically to find two rare transient classes — superluminous supernovae (host dwarf galaxies) and nuclear tidal disruption events — by fusing image cutouts with host-galaxy environmental features. Despite having only "a few tens of examples" of each rare class in training, it reaches average completeness of 73% (best 86%) for SLSNe and 80% (best 87%) for TDEs on an unseen ZTF Bright Transient Survey test set (~5,400 transients total). This is a single specialized model trained end-to-end on engineered features/images jointly, rather than a cascade conditioned on a prior generalist's output, but it is strong direct evidence that specialist architectures incorporating domain-specific side information can achieve high recall on rare classes from very few positive examples. — [arXiv:2312.04968](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.04968)
- "A hierarchical approach uses astrophysically motivated major features to separate different groups of classes, then proceeds down the classification hierarchy using classifiers that work best for each particular task" — general description of hierarchical transient-classification design philosophy, i.e., different classifier types/algorithms are matched to different branches of the taxonomy because "different types of classifiers perform better for different event classes." — [search synthesis citing transient classification literature, arXiv:2501.01496 context](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.01496)
- AnomalyMatch (arXiv:2505.03509) explicitly frames rare-object discovery as "an imbalanced binary classification problem" solved via semi-supervised learning (pseudo-labeling, consistency regularization) plus active learning (experts validate/label top-ranked candidates iteratively) — functioning as a continuously-retrained single-stage detector for the rare class rather than a two-model cascade, and demonstrating that this design can scale from 380,000 (Euclid Q1 jellyfish galaxies) to 99.6 million (Hubble Legacy Archive) sources while starting from "minimal labelled anomaly examples." — [arXiv:2505.03509](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.03509)
- Galaxy-merger detection: Pearson/Rodriguez-Gomez-style and follow-on work shows ensembling across different model families produces measurable gains. The "Mummi" (MUlti Model Merger Identifier) framework (arXiv:2407.18396) combines a CNN and a Vision Transformer as a jury-based ensemble and reports 95% "pure" classifications even at Gyr-long merger timescales, explicitly citing this as a way to address class imbalance. — [arXiv:2407.18396](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.18396)
- "Pitfalls of AI classification of rare objects: Galaxy Mergers" (arXiv:2302.11288) — located and confirmed as directly on-topic (title and abstract retrieved), but the PDF content could not be parsed by the fetch tool used in this session (binary/encoding issue), so specific quantitative claims from this paper could not be extracted and are left as a gap below rather than guessed at.
- Strong Lens Finding Challenge (arXiv:1802.03609, A&A 625, A119, 2019): across many independently-submitted methods, "several methods are able to identify upwards of half the lenses...without making a single false-positive identification" when thresholds are tuned on lensed-image brightness/size/contrast — illustrating that threshold/filter staging (even within a single classifier's score) is a recurring pattern for trading recall against purity in this subfield, and that multi-band ground-based data outperformed single-band space data, with multi-band space data expected to be best of all — a data/feature-staging insight relevant to any cascade design (what information should stage 1 vs. stage 2 see). — [arXiv:1802.03609](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1802.03609)

### Inferences
- Across astronomy sub-fields, "staging" is used far more often to manage **compute cost over huge datasets** (billions of sources down to thousands of candidates) than to manage **class rarity** via dedicated specialist models. Where rarity is specifically targeted, the dominant astronomy strategy today is a single specialized/hierarchical model with rarity-aware losses or richer input features (ORACLE, NEEDLE), or an ensemble of independently-trained *generalist* models whose combination improves coverage (Euclid Discovery Engine, DES comparative study, Mummi) — not a literal two-model generalist→specialist cascade gated on the generalist's own prediction.
- The reported benefits of these multi-model approaches are consistently about **completeness/recall** gains and **purity** control (e.g., DES study: fewer missed discoveries when combining searches; Euclid Discovery Engine: purity rises enormously above the ~0.05% random baseline), rather than compute efficiency, when the motivation is scientific coverage rather than triage.

### Gaps
- Could not extract the specific quantitative findings/recommendations of "Pitfalls of AI classification of rare objects: Galaxy Mergers" (arXiv:2302.11288) due to a PDF parsing failure; this paper's title/abstract match strongly suggests it contains relevant critiques (contamination, selection effects of ML-selected rare samples) and should be manually re-checked by the report writer if possible.
- No astronomy paper found runs a controlled comparison of "single end-to-end classifier" vs. "cascade/hierarchical classifier" at matched compute/data budget and reports a clean accuracy/recall delta attributable purely to the cascade structure; benefits reported are generally qualitative or conflated with other changes (more data, different architecture, ensembling across teams).

## What does the general ML literature say about cascade classifiers and mixture-of-experts architectures for rare/imbalanced class detection?

### Takeaway
Classical cascade-detector theory (Viola & Jones 2001 and descendants) establishes the core efficiency principle — reject easy negatives early, spend compute only on ambiguous cases — but was designed for speed, not for rare-positive recall, and its "reject early" philosophy is in tension with rare-class recall goals. Modern long-tailed/mixture-of-experts literature (Kang et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2021 "RIDE") is the more directly relevant modern analog, showing that decoupling representation learning from classification, and/or using multiple experts with diversity-promoting losses and routing, measurably improves tail-class performance over a single end-to-end classifier.

### Cited Findings
- Viola & Jones, "Rapid Object Detection using a Boosted Cascade of Simple Features," CVPR 2001 — introduces (1) the integral image for fast feature computation, (2) AdaBoost-selected weak classifiers, and (3) a cascade of increasingly complex classifiers where "if any classifier rejects the sub-window, no further processing is performed," enabling background regions to be discarded cheaply while more computation is spent on promising regions; achieved real-time face detection at 15 fps. Ranked as the most-influential CVPR 2001 paper. — [CVPR 2001 PDF](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~lowe/425/violaJones01.pdf)
- Bourdev & Brandt, "Robust Object Detection via Soft Cascade," CVPR 2005 — reformulates the hard-rejection cascade as a single boosted classifier with early-exit based on a partial-sum rejection threshold (a "rejection distribution vector"), improving on Viola-Jones's hard per-stage rejection; a documented critique of **all** prior cascade-learning approaches (including Viola-Jones) is that "none has a scheme to determine which examples are best to reject" — Viola-Jones initially tried to reject zero positive examples per stage until this became infeasible, then reluctantly allowed rejecting one positive at a time — i.e., the foundational cascade literature already flags that *stage-wise rejection of true positives is an unavoidable cost of cascading*, directly relevant to any lens-cascade design where a stage-1 "reject" on a true compound lens is unrecoverable downstream. — [Soft Cascade, CVPR 2005](https://mlanthology.org/cvpr/2005/bourdev2005cvpr-robust)
- Kang, Xie, Rohrbach, Yan, Gordo, Feng & Kalantidis, "Decoupling Representation and Classifier for Long-Tailed Recognition," ICLR 2020, arXiv:1910.09217 — shows that data imbalance is not necessarily harmful for learning good *representations*; representations learned via simple instance-balanced sampling, combined with *re-adjusting only the classifier* (not retraining the backbone) for the tail, achieves new state-of-the-art on ImageNet-LT, Places-LT, and iNaturalist, outperforming more complex re-sampling/re-weighting/memory-based methods. This directly supports a "generalist backbone + separately-tuned tail-aware decision layer" design pattern, as opposed to training one classifier end-to-end on imbalanced data. — [arXiv:1910.09217](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.09217)
- Wang, Lian, Miao, Liu & Yu, "Long-Tailed Recognition by Routing Diverse Distribution-Aware Experts" (RIDE), ICLR 2021 (spotlight), arXiv:2010.01809 — a mixture-of-experts architecture with three components: (1) multiple expert classifier heads sharing a backbone, (2) a **distribution-aware diversity loss** that explicitly encourages experts to disagree more on classes with fewer training instances (i.e., forcing specialization precisely where data is scarce), and (3) a **dynamic expert-routing module** that sends more ambiguous instances to additional experts at inference time (cheaper instances get fewer experts, harder/rarer-looking ones get more). Reports a 5–7% improvement over prior state-of-the-art on CIFAR100-LT, ImageNet-LT, and iNaturalist 2018, and is presented as a general framework compatible with different backbones and other long-tail training tricks. This is the closest general-ML analog to "generalist first pass, specialist second pass triggered by predicted difficulty/rarity" that was found in this research — the routing module's job is exactly to decide, per-input, how much specialist attention a case deserves. — [arXiv:2010.01809](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.01809)
- Astro-MoE (arXiv:2507.12611) and LightCurve MoE (arXiv:2305.13745) apply sparsely-gated mixture-of-experts transformer architectures to multiband astronomical time series, with a learnable gating function routing inputs to parallel experts "specialize[d] in different variability patterns," motivated by processing efficiency for LSST-scale survey volumes — showing the MoE/routing paradigm is actively being imported into astronomy time-domain classification, though these target general photometric classification efficiency rather than rare-class recall specifically. — [arXiv:2305.13745](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.13745); [arXiv:2507.12611](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.12611)
- General MoE gating-network behavior: "without modifications, noisy top-k gating models tend to collapse to using a small number of experts...as some experts perform slightly better due to random initialization, and gradient descent routes more examples to the best few experts" — a known training pathology (gate/expert collapse) relevant to any attempt to train a gate to route rare-class inputs to a specialist, since the rare class, by definition, provides the gate very little gradient signal to learn a distinct route for it. — [MoE gating network literature synthesis, cf. sparse MoE course materials](https://apxml.com/courses/mixture-of-experts-advanced-implementation/chapter-1-foundations-of-moe-models/gating-network-formulation)

### Inferences
- The "cascade" and "mixture-of-experts" traditions in general ML answer two different needs that both bear on the user's architecture: classical cascades (Viola-Jones, Bourdev-Brandt) optimize for **throughput** by rejecting easy negatives fast, at the acknowledged cost of occasionally rejecting true positives early and irrecoverably; modern long-tail MoE methods (Kang et al., RIDE) optimize for **tail-class accuracy** by either decoupling representation from classification or by explicitly routing harder/rarer cases to more/different capacity. A lens-finding cascade aimed at rescuing compound lenses is conceptually much closer to the RIDE/Kang lineage (rare-class accuracy) than to the Viola-Jones lineage (throughput), which has implications for design: it should probably avoid hard early rejection of ambiguous cases, and instead resemble RIDE's "route uncertain/rare-looking inputs to more model capacity" pattern or Kang et al.'s "shared backbone, specialized/adjusted classifier head" pattern, rather than a hard-reject triage cascade.
- RIDE's diversity loss is a plausible template for the lens use case: instead of training a single stage-2 specialist purely on the (very few) known compound lenses, one could train multiple expert heads on the shared generalist backbone with a diversity-promoting loss that rewards experts for disagreeing specifically on the kinds of inputs morphologically associated with compound/ring configurations, then route via confidence/uncertainty at inference time — this sidesteps needing the gate itself to have seen many rare positives.

### Gaps
- No general-ML paper was found that specifically addresses gating/routing where the rare class has only a handful (tens) of labeled examples, as opposed to "long-tailed but still hundreds-to-thousands of tail-class examples" (ImageNet-LT/iNaturalist-scale tails). The lens case (a few dozen known compound lenses) may be more extreme than most benchmarks used in RIDE/Kang-style long-tail literature, so transferability of their exact quantitative gains to the lens setting is uncertain and should be flagged as an assumption, not a validated result.

## Is there literature on routing/gating mechanisms that decide which "expert" model should handle an input based on predicted configuration type, and how is gating trained when the rare class has very few labeled examples?

### Takeaway
Classic sparse MoE gating (softmax/top-k over a learned linear or shallow function of the input) is well-studied and known to be prone to "expert collapse" under imbalance; RIDE (ICLR 2021) is the most relevant worked example of a diversity-promoting routing loss designed to keep rare classes from being starved of expert capacity; no literature was found that specifically solves the "few-shot gate" problem for a rare class with only a handful of positive examples, which the researcher flags as a specific, currently-open gap for the lens use case.

### Cited Findings
- Standard sparsely-gated MoE: "a linear gating network...outputs logits for each expert, applies softmax, and routes each token to the top-k experts (often k = 1 or 2)," typically "just a single linear layer applied to the token's input embedding, followed by a softmax function" — this is the default, simplest gating design pattern used across modern MoE literature. — [MoE gating network formulation reference](https://apxml.com/courses/mixture-of-experts-advanced-implementation/chapter-1-foundations-of-moe-models/gating-network-formulation)
- Statistical-mechanics analysis of mixture-of-experts training dynamics (Nowlan & Hinton lineage, NeurIPS/NIPS 1996 proceedings) describes a "symmetric phase" at low example counts where "experts are not specialized," with a transition to a "symmetry breaking phase" only once enough examples are seen for the gate to partition the input space effectively — directly implying that a gate asked to recognize a rare configuration type from very few examples may remain stuck in the unspecialized/symmetric regime rather than learning a meaningful rare-class route. — [NIPS 1996 proceedings](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/1176-statistical-mechanics-of-the-mixture-of-experts)
- Known MoE training pathology: "noisy top-k gating models tend to collapse to using a small number of experts instead of using all capacity, as some experts perform slightly better due to random initialization, and gradient descent routes more examples to the best few experts" — this collapse dynamic is a well-known risk factor specifically because gradient-based gate training is biased toward whichever expert already looks slightly better on the dominant (common) class, further starving a would-be rare-class expert of the routing signal needed to specialize. — [MoE training literature synthesis](https://apxml.com/courses/mixture-of-experts-advanced-implementation/chapter-1-foundations-of-moe-models/gating-network-formulation)
- RIDE (arXiv:2010.01809) sidesteps training a gate directly on rare-class labels by instead (a) training a shared backbone + multiple expert heads together with a diversity loss that is distribution-aware (i.e., explicitly weighted so disagreement is rewarded more on classes known in advance to be rare, from the *overall training-set class counts*, not from a separate few-shot gate-training step), and (b) using a lightweight routing module at inference that decides how many experts to consult based on input ambiguity, not based on having been shown many rare-class examples per se. This reframes the "few-shot gate" problem as "use known class-frequency statistics to bias a diversity loss," rather than "train a classifier to recognize rarity from few examples," which may be the more tractable framing for compound lenses. — [arXiv:2010.01809](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.01809)
- AnomalyMatch (arXiv:2505.03509) demonstrates a different route around the "few labeled rare examples" problem: rather than training a gate to classify configuration type, it uses semi-supervised pseudo-labeling plus active learning to expand the effective rare-class training set iteratively, starting from minimal labels, which could be viewed as a way to grow the stage-2 specialist's (or gate's) effective training data before fully committing to a fixed architecture. — [arXiv:2505.03509](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.03509)

### Inferences
- The lens-finding case (specialist = compound lenses, with order-of-tens of confirmed real examples) sits at a more extreme imbalance than most published MoE/long-tail routing work, which tends to assume tail classes with at least dozens-to-hundreds of examples in a large labeled set (ImageNet-LT tail classes, iNaturalist tail species). The literature suggests two viable adaptation strategies rather than a direct recipe: (1) do not train the gate end-to-end on rare-class labels at all — instead bias a diversity/uncertainty-based routing signal using known class-frequency priors (RIDE-style), or (2) grow the effective rare-class training set via semi-supervised/active learning before or alongside gate training (AnomalyMatch-style), likely combined with heavy use of realistic simulated compound lenses (as Wilde et al. 2022 already does for training) to give the gate more signal than the handful of confirmed real systems would alone provide.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that trains a gating/routing mechanism using primarily *simulated* rare-class examples (as opposed to real labeled examples) and then validates the gate's routing decisions against real rare examples — this is likely the most relevant missing piece for the lens use case, since compound lens training already leans heavily on simulations (Wilde et al. 2022), and whether a simulation-trained gate transfers to real rare-configuration recognition appears untested in the literature found.
- No quantitative comparison was found of "fixed rule-based routing" (e.g., route anything stage 1 scores as ambiguous, or anything with specific morphological features, to a specialist) versus "learned gate routing" specifically for a rare class with few real positives — this would be a useful ablation for the lens team to run themselves given the apparent absence of prior art.

## What are the known critiques or failure modes of cascade classifiers relevant to a team considering this architecture for lens finding?

### Takeaway
The central, well-documented failure mode across both classical object-detection cascades and modern retrieval/reranking cascades is that **a rejection (or omission from the candidate set) at an early stage is unrecoverable by any later stage** — i.e., "whatever stage 1 misses, stage 2 never sees" is not a hypothetical concern but the textbook-acknowledged core limitation of cascade/pipeline architectures, documented from the original Viola-Jones lineage through to modern two-stage retrieval-then-rerank systems.

### Cited Findings
- Bourdev & Brandt (CVPR 2005) explicitly document that in the Viola-Jones cascade lineage, "a key criticism of all previous cascade learning approaches is that none has a scheme to determine which examples are best to reject," and that Viola-Jones's own cascade "attempted to reject zero positive examples until this became impossible and then reluctantly gave up on one positive example at a time" — i.e., the foundational cascade-detector paper itself treats loss of true positives at early stages as an accepted, managed cost rather than something eliminable; it is managed via careful per-stage threshold tuning, not avoided outright. — [Soft Cascade, CVPR 2005](https://mlanthology.org/cvpr/2005/bourdev2005cvpr-robust)
- Modern two-stage retrieval/reranking literature states the same principle in different words: "items that do not reach the reranking window cannot be recovered, which establishes a fundamental upper bound on what downstream stages can achieve," and "the reranker can fix ordering within the candidates, but it can't recover a document that wasn't surfaced" — with the explicit framing that "Recall@[first-stage-cutoff] is the silent ceiling" on the whole pipeline, and that this failure is dangerous precisely because "the pipeline still looks healthier because the top slot improved, but the failure is invisible in exactly the metric people watch." — [The Recall Ceiling of LLM Recommendation Reranking, arXiv:2609.27953](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.27953); [First-Pass Retrieval reference](https://www.zeroentropy.dev/concepts/first-pass-retrieval/)
- Generalizing beyond retrieval: analysis of cascaded inference systems notes that "the second stage's effectiveness depends on how often it independently judges cases rather than simply replicating the first stage's behavior—if it always behaves like the first stage, the cascade degenerates to just the first stage," and that for a cascade to add value, "the second stage must differ from the first along a meaningful axis (different model, different evidence modality, or rule-based checks)" — otherwise the architecture provides only added latency/complexity with no accuracy benefit. — [cascade effectiveness analysis](https://theaiengineer.substack.com/p/what-is-a-reranker/comments)
- Safety-aware cascaded inference work (arXiv:2607.25468, applied to crop damage assessment, a different rare-event-adjacent domain) frames a related positive counter-finding: a well-designed cascade "can convert input uncertainty into routing escalation rather than prediction error" (i.e., instead of silently misclassifying an ambiguous case, the system escalates it to a later/human stage) — but explicitly caveats that "this safety guarantee is contingent on later stages maintaining their diagnostic reliability under the same conditions that caused the earlier stage to fail," meaning the escalation-based mitigation only works if stage 1's uncertainty signal is itself reliable and if stage 2 is not subject to the same blind spots as stage 1. — [Safety-Aware Cascaded Inference, arXiv:2607.25468](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.25468)
- Directly in the lens-finding domain, Wilde et al. (2022) already demonstrate the precursor condition that makes this critique sharply relevant: a generalist CNN's recall on compound arcs (75%) and double rings (56%, OU-200 numbers) is already well below its recall on ordinary lenses — so if a hypothetical stage-1 generalist is used as the gatekeeper that decides which images even reach a stage-2 compound-lens specialist, roughly a quarter to nearly half of true compound/ring lenses risk being filtered out *before* the specialist ever gets a chance to evaluate them, reproducing exactly the "recall ceiling" failure mode documented in the general cascade literature. — [arXiv:2202.12776](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2202.12776)

### Inferences
- The single most important design implication from this literature for the lens-finding team: if a stage-1 generalist classifier is used as a hard gate (i.e., only images that pass some stage-1 lens/non-lens threshold are ever shown to a stage-2 compound-lens specialist), the overall pipeline's recall on compound lenses is capped at stage 1's recall on compound lenses (already measured at only 52–76% in Wilde et al. 2022) — the specialist cannot improve on cases stage 1 silently drops. This argues strongly for either (a) setting a very permissive/low stage-1 threshold specifically for what gets forwarded to the specialist stage (accepting many more false positives into stage 2 to protect compound-lens recall), (b) running the specialist in parallel on the *full* input stream rather than gated on stage-1's output, or (c) using stage-1's *uncertainty* (not just its top prediction) as the routing signal, consistent with the "escalate uncertainty rather than discard it" pattern highlighted in the crop-damage cascade safety paper.
- The literature also warns that a stage-2 specialist only adds value if it genuinely differs from stage 1 "along a meaningful axis" — for the lens case, this likely means the specialist should use different evidence (e.g., explicit multi-plane lens-model fitting, different photometric bands, or morphological features like multiple distinct arc systems/image multiplicities) rather than simply being "the same CNN retrained on more compound-lens examples," or the cascade risks degenerating into the generalist alone with extra latency.
