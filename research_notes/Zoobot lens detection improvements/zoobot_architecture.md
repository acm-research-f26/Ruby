# Zoobot Architecture and Its Adaptation for Strong Gravitational Lens Finding

## What backbones does Zoobot ship, what checkpoints are primary, and what was it trained on?

### Takeaway
Zoobot (Walmsley et al.) is a library of pretrained CNN/ViT-hybrid encoders — ConvNeXt, MaxViT, ResNet, and EfficientNet families — trained as multi-task classifiers to answer the full Galaxy Zoo decision-tree taxonomy on the ~820k-image, 100M+-vote "GZ Evo" compilation spanning multiple surveys (GZ2, GZ Hubble, GZ CANDELS, GZ DECaLS/DESI, GZ Cosmic Dawn/HSC); the project's own documentation recommends **ConvNeXt-Nano** as the default/practical checkpoint and ConvNeXt-Small/Base or MaxViT-Base when more accuracy and GPU budget are available.

### Cited Findings
- Zoobot's documented pretrained-model zoo includes four backbone families: **ConvNeXt** (Pico 9.1M, Nano 15.6M, Tiny 44.6M, Small 58.5M, Base 88.6M, Large 197.8M params), **MaxViT** (Tiny 29.1M, Small 64.9M, Base 124.5M, Large 211.8M params), **ResNet** (18, 50, 101), and **EfficientNet** (B0 5.33M, V2-S 48.3M) — [Zoobot Pretrained Models docs](https://zoobot.readthedocs.io/en/latest/pretrained_models.html)
- The docs explicitly recommend: *"We suggest starting with ConvNeXT-Nano for most users"* for single-GPU practicality, while ConvNeXT-Small, ConvNeXT-Base, and MaxViT-Base are recommended "for maximum accuracy" on high-end GPUs — [Zoobot Pretrained Models docs](https://zoobot.readthedocs.io/en/latest/pretrained_models.html)
- Reported test-loss benchmarks range from 19.05 (ConvNeXT-Base, best) to 19.83 (ResNet18, worst); ConvNeXt and MaxViT variants consistently beat ResNet and EfficientNet on these benchmarks — [Zoobot Pretrained Models docs](https://zoobot.readthedocs.io/en/latest/pretrained_models.html)
- All of these checkpoints are trained on the **GZ Evo** dataset: "820k images and 100M+ volunteer votes" drawn from GZ2, GZ UKIDSS, GZ Hubble, GZ CANDELS, GZ DECaLS/DESI, and GZ Cosmic Dawn (HSC) — [Zoobot Pretrained Models docs](https://zoobot.readthedocs.io/en/latest/pretrained_models.html)
- The foundational scaling-law study behind the "Zoobot 2.0" release, **Walmsley et al. 2024, "Scaling Laws for Galaxy Images"** (arXiv:2404.02973, cs.CV/astro-ph.GA, submitted 3 Apr 2024, 20 co-authors led by Walmsley), performed "the first systematic investigation of supervised scaling laws outside of an ImageNet-like context" using 840k galaxy images and 100M+ Galaxy Zoo annotations, and reports an average 31% relative error-rate reduction across 5 downstream tasks from scaling — [arXiv:2404.02973](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02973)
- That paper states scaling data follows a power law across all architectures/tasks, but scaling parameter count only helps on some ("more subjectively challenging") tasks, and explicitly cautions that "scaling alone is not sufficient to address our domain gap" — practitioners with visually distinct images are advised to pursue "in-domain adaptation followed by targeted downstream labelling" rather than relying on scale alone — [arXiv:2404.02973](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02973)
- The core software/methods paper is **Walmsley et al. 2023, "Zoobot: Adaptable Deep Learning Models for Galaxy Morphology,"** *Journal of Open Source Software*, 8(85), 5312, submitted 9 Mar 2023, published 8 May 2023, DOI 10.21105/joss.05312, with co-authors including Campbell Allen, Ben Aussel, Micah Bowles, and others — [JOSS 10.21105/joss.05312](https://joss.theoj.org/papers/10.21105/joss.05312)
- Zoobot's GitHub README describes training data additionally including Galaxy Zoo DESI (~8.7M galaxies classified), Hubble, and CANDELS imagery, and lists an "Astronomaly at Scale: Searching for Anomalies Amongst 4 Million Galaxies" paper among its cited downstream applications — [github.com/mwalmsley/zoobot](https://github.com/mwalmsley/zoobot)
- An underlying architecture precedent, **Walmsley et al. 2022, MNRAS 513(2), 1581–1599, "Practical galaxy morphology tools from deep supervised representation learning,"** showed that a model trained to answer every Galaxy Zoo DECaLS question learns transferable semantic representations useful for tasks it was never trained on (similarity search, personalized anomaly recommendation) — [search result summary citing MNRAS 513(2) 1581-1599]

### Inferences
- Zoobot's design philosophy (one multi-headed model trained to predict the entire GZ decision-tree vote distribution at once, rather than a single binary label) is what makes it reusable as a generic "galaxy foundation model" — the pretraining task is deliberately broad morphology, not any lensing-specific signal.
- Because the recommended default (ConvNeXt-Nano) is a relatively small, efficient backbone chosen for accessibility rather than maximum fidelity, any lens-finding fine-tune built on the "recommended" checkpoint is working from a capacity-constrained encoder unless the larger ConvNeXt-Base/MaxViT-Base checkpoints are explicitly substituted.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the full-text GZ Evo/DESI component counts (e.g., exact split of volunteer votes per survey) beyond the aggregate "820k images / 100M+ votes" headline figure; the JOSS paper's full text (architecture diagrams, loss function definitions) was not retrievable in this pass beyond its citation metadata.
- I found no explicit statement in Zoobot documentation of a Euclid-specific pretrained checkpoint being shipped by the Zoobot project itself (as opposed to being fine-tuned by downstream Euclid-collaboration users, see below) — this should be treated as unconfirmed rather than ruled out.

## Has Zoobot (or a similar morphology foundation model) been used specifically for strong-lens finding, and with what results?

### Takeaway
Yes — Zoobot was fine-tuned and used as one of five competing machine-learning lens-finding networks in the **Euclid Quick Release 1 (Q1) "Strong Lensing Discovery Engine"** series, and it was reported as **the best-performing of the five networks**, with a documented fine-tuning recipe (partial-layer unfreezing of a ConvNeXt-Nano backbone) and quantified grade-A/B/C lens recovery statistics.

### Cited Findings
- The relevant paper is **Euclid Collaboration / N. E. P. Lines, T. E. Collett, M. Walmsley et al., "Euclid Quick Data Release (Q1). The Strong Lensing Discovery Engine C: Finding lenses with machine learning"**, arXiv:2503.15326v2 [astro-ph.GA], first submitted 19 Mar 2025, revised 26 Jun 2025, part of the Q1 series (companion papers include arXiv:2503.15310 "First visual morphology catalogue" and arXiv:2503.15324 "...Discovery Engine A — system overview and lens catalogue") — [arXiv:2503.15326](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.15326)
- Five ML models were benchmarked on ~1 million Q1 objects: **Model 1** (a custom four-layer CNN), **Model 2 "OU-100"**, **Model 3 "IncNet"** (Inception-based), **Model 4 = Zoobot** (the fine-tuned pretrained morphology classifier), and **Model 5 "LensVision"** — [arXiv:2503.15326](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.15326)
- Zoobot's architecture adaptation: researchers took the **ConvNeXt-Nano (15.6M-parameter)** Zoobot checkpoint and froze all parameters except the final classification head plus the ConvNeXt blocks immediately preceding it; they report that fine-tuning too few layers "requires the classifier to closely resemble the original Zoobot classifier, which is not designed for lens finding," while fine-tuning too many "erases more of the useful [pretrained] features" — the optimum was unfreezing exactly the head plus the preceding block(s). Training used single-channel (IE-band) greyscale images with arcsinh scaling, batch size 64, learning rate 10⁻⁴ — [arXiv:2503.15326](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.15326)
- Reported recovery on the top 1000 ranked objects (per model): Zoobot — 122 grade-A, 41 grade-B, 67 grade-C, 770 non-lenses; four-layer CNN — 69 A / 34 B; OU-100 — 24 A / 12 B; IncNet — 8 A / 5 B; LensVision — 3 A / 4 B. Zoobot was explicitly the top performer of the five — [arXiv:2503.15326](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.15326)
- Combining all five networks' rankings with citizen-science (Space Warps) and expert visual inspection yielded 250 grade-A and 247 grade-B lenses from Q1's 63 deg²; extrapolated to the full Euclid survey, the top-ranked 1 million images are projected to contain ~75,000 grade-A or B strong lenses — [arXiv:2503.15326](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.15326); consistent top-line numbers (497 lenses total, 250 grade-A, 243 previously unpublished) also appear in the companion Discovery Engine A paper, arXiv:2503.15324 — [arXiv:2503.15324 search summary](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.15324)
- Zoobot's purity/completeness trade-off is explicitly quantified: *"an almost entirely pure sample of 35% of the lenses, but the purity falls to 20% when recovering up to 60% of the known lenses"*; achieving 100% true-positive rate required inspecting the top 10% of the full dataset. Even in the top 1000 ranked objects, true lenses remained a minority (~77% false-positive rate among the highest-confidence predictions) — [arXiv:2503.15326](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.15326)
- Failure-mode analysis: Zoobot's top-100 false positives were dominated by "chance alignment of galaxies" (interloper pairs mistaken for arc+lens configurations), not image artifacts (only 2 artifacts in the top 100). Edge-on disc lenses ranked poorly ("visually appear quite different to the average lens, especially at high inclination"). Of four known double-source-plane lenses (DSPLs), two ranked in the top 10 by Zoobot but two others ranked 1902 and 2349 — the lowest-ranked had "smaller Einstein radii, fainter arcs, or lens light blending with the arc light." Bayesian inference estimated Zoobot missed 45⁺⁴³₋₂₆ (68% CI) grade-A lenses across the full ~1M-object Q1 dataset — [arXiv:2503.15326](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.15326)
- The authors attribute Zoobot's (and transfer-learning generally) advantage over the bespoke/from-scratch networks to: (1) needing far less labelled training data (~10³ examples vs. 10⁴–10⁵ for from-scratch nets), (2) reduced overfitting via already-learned generic morphological features, and (3) avoiding spurious shortcut learning on simulated-lens artifacts (e.g., PSF orientation cues) that from-scratch networks trained on simulations can latch onto — [arXiv:2503.15326](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.15326)
- Interestingly, the simpler four-layer CNN (Model 1) complemented Zoobot: it ranked all four known DSPLs in the top 500, substantially better than Zoobot on that specific rare sub-class — the authors read this as evidence that different architectures capture complementary morphological information, motivating ensembling rather than single-model reliance — [arXiv:2503.15326](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.15326)
- All five teams chose greyscale, single-band (IE) images over color inputs despite having the option; Zoobot's team found arcsinh stretch outperformed MTF (midtone transfer function) scaling, while another team (Model 2) found the opposite — the paper notes this suggests "machine-learning models prioritise different aspects of images when classifying lenses" than human inspectors — [arXiv:2503.15326](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.15326)
- A separate, later Euclid Q1 paper, **Euclid Collaboration / N. E. P. Lines et al., "Euclid Quick Data Release (Q1). From simulations to sky: Advancing machine-learning lens detection with real Euclid data"**, arXiv:2512.05899, submitted 5 Dec 2025, reports that networks trained purely on simulated lenses reach 92% completeness at 100% purity on simulated test data but only **50% completeness at 24% purity on real Euclid data** — a stark sim-to-real domain gap — and that adding real lenses/non-lens hard negatives to training improves completeness by 25–30 percentage points — [arXiv:2512.05899 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.05899)

### Inferences
- The partial-unfreezing fine-tune strategy used for Zoobot in the Discovery Engine C paper is a direct, documented instantiation of exactly the kind of "architecture modification / head design" the research objective asks about: it keeps the ConvNeXt-Nano trunk's early/mid layers frozen (preserving generic morphology features) and retrains only the head plus the last block(s) on a much smaller lens/non-lens labelled set.
- The Q1 results constitute positive evidence that a Galaxy-Zoo-pretrained morphology encoder transfers usefully to lens finding and can outperform bespoke from-scratch lens-finding CNNs on overall grade-A/B recovery, but the data also show it is not uniformly best (it underperforms a simpler CNN specifically on the rare DSPL subclass, and it has a high false-positive floor on faint/small-Einstein-radius arcs) — consistent with the idea that thin, faint, small-scale arc structure remains a weak point even for the best-performing network in this benchmark.
- The 2512.05899 sim-to-real domain-gap finding (92%→50% completeness) was reported without mention of Zoobot in the retrievable abstract; it is unclear whether Zoobot specifically was part of that follow-up study's test set of models, so this should not be assumed to be about Zoobot's performance specifically.

### Gaps
- I did not find any peer-reviewed or arXiv paper applying Zoobot to lens finding *outside* the Euclid Q1 Discovery Engine series (i.e., no independent group outside the Euclid Collaboration reporting a Zoobot-backbone lens classifier was found in this search pass). This may exist but was not located within the search budget.
- I was not able to confirm from the retrievable abstract of arXiv:2512.05899 whether Zoobot was among the models re-evaluated in that sim-to-real follow-up study, or whether its specific real-data completeness/purity numbers were reported there — flagged as unverified rather than asserted either way.
- No explicit precision/recall/F1/AUC numbers were reported for Zoobot in the Discovery Engine C paper (the paper uses grade-based lens counts and purity/completeness curves rather than standard ML classification metrics); readers wanting strict precision/recall should treat the purity/completeness figures above as the closest available proxy.

## Is there published critique of Zoobot's (or ConvNeXt/ViT-family) architecture relevant to thin, faint, high-contrast arc-like features?

### Takeaway
No paper was found that specifically critiques Zoobot's ConvNeXt patchify stem or downsampling path as a cause of missed lensed arcs; the closest documented evidence is the Discovery Engine C paper's own failure-mode analysis (above), which empirically shows Zoobot/ConvNeXt-Nano struggles most with faint arcs, small Einstein radii, and lens-light-blended arcs — but this is reported as an empirical finding, not traced to a specific architectural mechanism (e.g., patchify stem or pooling resolution loss) in the text I could retrieve.

### Cited Findings
- The Discovery Engine C paper's own error analysis (see above) identifies that Zoobot's weakest cases are lenses with "smaller Einstein radii, fainter arcs, or lens light blending with the arc light," and that edge-on disc lenses (atypical morphology) also rank poorly — [arXiv:2503.15326](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.15326). This is a direct, if qualitative, empirical signal that the architecture (as fine-tuned) is weaker on exactly the thin/faint/high-contrast-arc regime the research objective flags, but the paper does not attribute this to a specific architectural component (patchify stem, stride, receptive field, etc.).
- A general (non-Zoobot-specific) methodological critique, **"Systematic biases when using deep neural networks for annotating large catalogs of astronomical images"** (arXiv:2201.03131), documents that DCNNs used for astronomical image annotation act as "black boxes" whose behavior is sensitive to training-data context (e.g., sky location of training galaxies affects classification behavior for basic elliptical/spiral classification) — this is a general architecture-agnostic bias caveat, not specific to ConvNeXt or to arc/thin-feature detection — [arXiv:2201.03131](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.03131)
- General CNN literature describes two inductive biases relevant to this question: translation invariance and hierarchical local structure (nearby pixels more related than distant ones) — properties that, in principle, could disadvantage detection of large-scale, spatially extended, low-surface-brightness curved structures (like Einstein rings/arcs) relative to compact, high-contrast point-like features, though I found no paper making this argument explicitly in the lensing context — [general CNN architecture search summary, uncited specific paper]
- Generic (non-astronomy) computer-vision literature on ConvNeXt's patchify stem (a non-overlapping 4×4 stride-4 convolution replacing the ResNet-style 7×7 stride-2 conv + maxpool) notes this changes early-layer receptive-field/overlap behavior, but general object-detection benchmarking literature found in this search reports ConvNeXt-T backbones perform comparably or well on small-object detection in standard (non-astronomical) benchmarks, with any degradation attributed to downstream detection-neck kernel sizes rather than the stem itself — [general ConvNeXt search summary, non-astronomy sources]

### Inferences
- The absence of a dedicated paper diagnosing *why* ConvNeXt-family architectures (or Zoobot specifically) under-detect thin arcs — as opposed to simply reporting that they do, empirically, in the Discovery Engine C results — suggests this is an open/under-studied question in the lens-finding literature as of this search (Oct 2026), rather than a settled result. The Discovery Engine C findings should be read as suggestive evidence, not a architecture-attributed causal mechanism.
- Given that the Euclid team's own fine-tuning ablation found "fine-tuning too many layers erases more of the useful features" while "too few" leaves the head too close to its original (non-lensing) task, there is an implicit acknowledgment that Zoobot's learned generic-morphology representation is not naturally well-aligned to the rare, thin, high-spatial-frequency arc signal — hence the need for careful partial-unfreezing rather than naive full fine-tuning or head-only linear probing.

### Gaps
- I could not locate any paper that explicitly discusses "downsampling artifacts" or "patchify stem" as a named cause of missed lensed arcs — this specific mechanistic critique appears to be absent from the literature I could access, or at least not indexed under searchable terms within this session's budget. This should be reported as a gap, not inferred as "no such critique exists."
- I did not find CNN/ViT-vs-thin-curved-feature bias papers specific to astronomical imaging (e.g., comparing performance on arcs vs. compact sources). The general CNN inductive-bias discussion found is from generic computer-vision sources, not astronomy-specific, and was not strongly sourced to a single citable paper in this pass.

## What does Zoobot's documentation say about intended downstream use, and its known strengths/weaknesses for anomaly or rare-feature detection?

### Takeaway
Zoobot's own documentation and the broader Walmsley publication record explicitly position it as a general-purpose finetuning backbone (binary/multiclass classification, regression, full GZ decision-tree prediction) and separately position its learned *representations* (not the classifier head) as useful for rare/anomalous galaxy discovery and similarity search — but these are two distinct, documented use cases, and the rare-feature use case is about generic morphological outliers rather than a tiny, spatially localized feature like a lensed arc superimposed on an otherwise ordinary galaxy.

### Cited Findings
- The Zoobot GitHub README's quickstart/finetuning guide demonstrates binary classification (example: "finding ringed galaxies"), multi-class morphology tasks, regression, and full decision-tree counting as representative downstream finetuning scenarios — [github.com/mwalmsley/zoobot](https://github.com/mwalmsley/zoobot); [zoobot.readthedocs.io/en/latest/guides/finetuning.html](https://zoobot.readthedocs.io/en/latest/guides/finetuning.html)
- The JOSS software paper (Walmsley et al. 2023, JOSS 8(85):5312) frames Zoobot explicitly for "astronomers who want to solve a galaxy image task such as finding merging galaxies or counting spiral arms" via finetuning pretrained models — [JOSS 10.21105/joss.05312](https://joss.theoj.org/papers/10.21105/joss.05312)
- For rare/anomalous feature discovery specifically, **Walmsley & Scaife, "Rare Galaxy Classes Identified In Foundation Model Representations"** (arXiv:2312.02910), accepted at the NeurIPS 2023 Machine Learning and the Physical Sciences workshop, uses Zoobot's learned representation space (not a finetuned classifier head) to find structure "beyond those needed to predict the pretraining labels," applying a clustering approach to isolate "groups of galaxies with rare and scientifically-interesting morphologies" — [arXiv:2312.02910](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.02910)
- Zoobot's README cites "Astronomaly at Scale: Searching for Anomalies Amongst 4 Million Galaxies" among its linked downstream-application papers, indicating the project explicitly tracks and supports anomaly-detection use of its representations — [github.com/mwalmsley/zoobot](https://github.com/mwalmsley/zoobot)
- Walmsley et al. 2022 (MNRAS 513(2), 1581–1599), "Practical galaxy morphology tools from deep supervised representation learning," is cited as showing that a multi-survey pretrained model's extracted features are useful for "similarity search (finding similar galaxies to a query galaxy), personalised anomaly recommendation (finding galaxies interesting to a specific researcher)" — i.e., representation-level rare/anomalous-object retrieval, as opposed to pixel-level rare-feature (e.g., small arc) segmentation — [search summary citing MNRAS 513(2), 1581-1599]
- The Scaling Laws paper (arXiv:2404.02973) explicitly frames the limits of this approach: scaling the pretraining data/model alone is not sufficient to close domain gaps, and visually distinct target tasks benefit more from "in-domain adaptation followed by targeted downstream labelling" than from the foundation model alone — directly relevant to lens finding, since lensed arcs are a visually distinct, rare signal relative to the GZ Evo pretraining distribution — [arXiv:2404.02973](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02973)

### Inferences
- There is a meaningful distinction, confirmed across sources, between Zoobot's two documented "rare feature" capabilities: (1) whole-galaxy-level anomaly/rarity in representation space (an image-level outlier, e.g., an unusual morphology class), versus (2) a small, localized, rare sub-image feature superimposed on an otherwise normal-looking galaxy (a lensed arc). The published rare-galaxy-discovery work (Walmsley & Scaife 2023) addresses the former; the Discovery Engine C fine-tuning work addresses the latter via supervised finetuning rather than unsupervised representation clustering. No paper found in this search combines the two (i.e., using Zoobot's unsupervised representation-clustering/anomaly approach, rather than supervised finetuning, specifically to surface lensed-arc anomalies).
- Zoobot's own scaling-law paper's explicit caveat — that scale alone can't close domain gaps for visually distinct tasks — is consistent with, and arguably predicts, the empirical struggle with faint/small-Einstein-radius arcs reported in the Discovery Engine C fine-tuning results: lensed arcs are exactly the kind of "visually distinct" rare signal the authors flag as requiring targeted labelled data rather than reliance on the foundation model's generic morphology features alone.

### Gaps
- I found no explicit statement, in any Zoobot documentation or paper, warning users that Zoobot/ConvNeXt-based encoders are weak at detecting small, thin, spatially localized sub-image features (like arcs) as opposed to whole-image/whole-galaxy classification; this absence should not be read as the documentation claiming it handles such features well — it is simply not discussed as a limitation in the Zoobot-authored sources I could access.
- I did not find a direct statement from Walmsley or the Zoobot documentation explicitly discussing strong lensing or arc-finding as an anticipated/endorsed use case prior to the Euclid Collaboration's own Q1 fine-tuning work — the lens-finding application appears to originate from the Euclid Discovery Engine team's choice to benchmark Zoobot alongside bespoke networks, not from a Zoobot-team-authored lensing-specific paper.
