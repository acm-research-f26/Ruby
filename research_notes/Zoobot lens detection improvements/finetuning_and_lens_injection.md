# Transfer-Learning / Fine-Tuning Strategies and Lens Injection for Gravitational Lens Detection

## Key Question 1: What does the transfer-learning literature say about layer-wise unfreezing and discriminative/layer-wise learning rates when fine-tuning a pretrained CNN/ViT, in general ML and in astronomy?

### Takeaway
General ML literature (ULMFiT, Yosinski et al.) established that lower/early layers encode general features and upper/later layers encode task-specific features, motivating gradual, top-down unfreezing with lower learning rates on earlier layers; the astronomy-specific example (Zoobot/Walmsley et al. 2021) operationalizes this exactly, testing 6 discrete unfreezing depths with a ~100x learning-rate drop upon unfreezing and permanently frozen batch-norm layers.

### Cited Findings
- Howard & Ruder (2018), "Universal Language Model Fine-tuning for Text Classification" (ULMFiT), arXiv:1801.06146 — introduces **discriminative fine-tuning** (different, smaller learning rates for lower/earlier layers since they hold more general information) and **gradual unfreezing** (layers unfrozen progressively from the last/top layer to the first/bottom layer, one layer per epoch, to avoid catastrophic forgetting of general low-level representations), plus slanted triangular learning rates. — [arXiv:1801.06146](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.06146v2)
- Howard & Ruder (2018) report ULMFiT reduces error by 18–24% on most of six text-classification datasets versus prior state of the art, and that with only 100 labeled examples it matches the performance of training from scratch with 100× more data — demonstrating the general sample-efficiency payoff of staged fine-tuning. — [arXiv:1801.06146](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.06146v2)
- Yosinski, Clune, Bengio & Lipson (2014), "How transferable are features in deep neural networks?", NeurIPS 2014 / arXiv:1411.1792 — shows CNNs trained on natural images learn Gabor-filter/color-blob-like general features in early layers, transitioning to task-specific features in later layers; transferability degrades as the semantic distance between source and target task grows, but features copied from almost any depth and then fine-tuned still give a generalization boost over training from scratch, and fine-tuning (vs. freezing) consistently helps when enough target data exists. — [arXiv:1411.1792](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1411.1792)
- Kumar, Raghunathan, Jones, Ma & Liang (2022), "Fine-Tuning can Distort Pretrained Features and Underperform Out-of-Distribution", ICLR 2022 / arXiv:2202.10054 — across 10 distribution-shift benchmarks (BREEDS-Living17, BREEDS-Entity30, DomainNet, CIFAR→STL, CIFAR-10.1, FMoW, ImageNetV2, ImageNet-R, ImageNet-A, ImageNet-Sketch), full fine-tuning achieves ~2 percentage points higher in-distribution accuracy than linear probing but ~7 points *lower* out-of-distribution accuracy on average, because fine-tuning the head and the backbone simultaneously distorts pretrained low-level features. — [arXiv:2202.10054](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.10054v1)
- Kumar et al. (2022) propose and validate "LP-FT" (linear-probe-then-fine-tune: train the head first with the backbone frozen, then unfreeze and fine-tune everything), which beats both full fine-tuning and linear probing alone — about 1 point better in-distribution and 10 points better out-of-distribution than full fine-tuning. — [arXiv:2202.10054](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.10054v1)
- Astronomy-specific example — Walmsley et al. (2021/2022), "Practical Galaxy Morphology Tools from Deep Supervised Representation Learning" (Zoobot), arXiv:2110.12735 — directly implements this ML-literature prescription for a downstream ring-galaxy-identification task: adds a lightweight new head (two 64-unit dense layers with dropout p=0.75 and ReLU, final 1-unit sigmoid layer) atop the frozen pretrained EfficientNet backbone; trains the head alone first at learning rate 1e-3 (Adam); then **progressively unfreezes** from the top, testing six discrete depths — "top block only," then blocks 7+, 6+, 5+, 4+, and finally all blocks — each stage fine-tuned at a much lower learning rate of 1e-5; batch-normalization layers are **never** unfrozen at any stage. — [arXiv:2110.12735](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.12735)
- Walmsley et al. find that fine-tuning depth matters differently depending on the pretrained source: for the galaxy-pretrained (GZ DECaLS) backbone, unfreezing all layers only improves accuracy by roughly 5–10 percentage points over the frozen/head-only model, whereas for an ImageNet-pretrained backbone, unfreezing "the intermediate blocks and above" is necessary to go from ~70% to ~85% accuracy — i.e., a domain-relevant pretrained backbone needs much less unfreezing to reach high performance than a generic (ImageNet) one. — [arXiv:2110.12735](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.12735)

### Inferences
- The consistent prescription across both the general-ML and astronomy-specific sources is: start with a frozen backbone + trainable head (cheapest, least risk of overfitting/forgetting), and only progressively unfreeze deeper layers — with correspondingly smaller learning rates — as more labeled target-task data becomes available or as frozen-backbone performance plateaus below what is needed.
- The Kumar et al. (2022) LP-FT result suggests that for a lens-detection task where the real/sky data distribution differs from the simulated training distribution (a sim-to-real distribution shift), naive full fine-tuning on simulations risks being *worse* out-of-distribution (on real sky data) than a disciplined linear-probe-first-then-fine-tune schedule — directly relevant to Zoobot-for-lens-detection work, which faces exactly this simulation/real distribution shift.

### Gaps
- No astronomy-specific paper was found that explicitly cites ULMFiT or uses the term "discriminative fine-tuning"/"layer decay" by name; the Zoobot paper independently arrives at an equivalent gradual-unfreezing + reduced-learning-rate protocol without citing the NLP lineage. This connection is an inference, not a found citation.

## Key Question 2: How much labeled data is typically reported as needed to safely fine-tune deeper layers vs. train only a linear probe/head, in general transfer learning and in astronomical imaging?

### Takeaway
General-purpose guidance (and the Kumar et al. result) is that linear probing wins with very small label counts (sources discuss regimes as low as ~16 examples/class) while full/deep fine-tuning becomes competitive or better once label counts reach the hundreds per class; the astronomy-specific Zoobot and Domínguez-Sánchez results are consistent with this, showing order-of-magnitude reductions (from ~10⁴–10⁵ to ~10²–10³ labeled examples) when the pretrained backbone is domain-relevant.
### Cited Findings
- General ML guidance (secondary summary, from search aggregation of CNN fine-tuning tutorials/coursework): with very little data, use a linear probe on a frozen pretrained model; with moderate data, fine-tune; with very large datasets, either fine-tuning or training from scratch is viable. One concrete example cited: with 16 examples per class, a linear probe from an ImageNet-pretrained model performs best, while with 400+ examples per class, full fine-tuning from ImageNet becomes more competitive. — [search aggregation, exact primary source not confirmed — see Gaps](https://www.catalyzex.com/paper/fine-tuning-can-distort-pretrained-features)
- Kumar et al. (2022), arXiv:2202.10054 — formal evidence that fine-tuning's advantage over linear probing is conditional on in-distribution target performance and target-set size; under large distribution shift, fine-tuning can underperform linear probing out-of-distribution regardless of target data volume, motivating the LP-FT two-stage schedule as a safer default. — [arXiv:2202.10054](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.10054v1)
- Astronomy-specific: Walmsley et al. (2021/2022), Zoobot, arXiv:2110.12735 — for the ring-galaxy downstream task: with fewer than 10 labeled ring examples, all models (from-scratch, ImageNet-pretrained, DECaLS/Zoobot-pretrained) "struggle similarly"; between 10 and 100 labeled rings, the galaxy-pretrained (DECaLS) model "vastly outperforms" ImageNet-pretrained and from-scratch models; between 100 and 1,000 rings, ImageNet-fine-tuned models improve but the galaxy-pretrained model remains clearly ahead; with the full available set of 6,962 labeled rings, all approaches converge closer together (approximate accuracies: DECaLS-pretrained ~95%, ImageNet-pretrained ~85%, from-scratch ~90%). — [arXiv:2110.12735](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.12735)
- Walmsley et al. estimate that training an equivalent model **from scratch** (no pretraining) would require roughly 10⁴–10⁵ labeled ring examples, corresponding to having to visually label on the order of 10⁵–10⁶ galaxies — i.e., pretraining plus fine-tuning cuts the required labeled-example count by roughly 1–3 orders of magnitude for this task. — [arXiv:2110.12735](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.12735)
- Domínguez Sánchez et al. (2019), "Transfer learning for galaxy morphology from one survey to another", MNRAS / arXiv:1807.00807 — a CNN trained from scratch on one survey (SDSS) typically needs on the order of 5,000 pre-labeled galaxies; using transfer learning (reusing SDSS-trained weights and fine-tuning), a "fast domain adaptation" step using only ~300–500 galaxies from the new survey (DES) is enough to reach >95% accuracy with significantly improved completeness and purity — transfer learning reduces the needed labeled sample by "almost one order of magnitude." — [arXiv:1807.00807](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1807.00807)

### Inferences
- Both the general-ML and astronomy-specific evidence converge on the same qualitative rule of thumb: tens of labels only support a frozen/linear-probe head; hundreds of labels support shallow/partial unfreezing or fast domain adaptation with a related pretrained backbone; thousands-plus labels are needed to safely fine-tune most/all layers or to match a domain-relevant pretrained model by training from scratch.
- For a lens-detection task starting from Zoobot (galaxy-morphology-pretrained, hence domain-relevant for galaxy images but not pretrained on lensing-specific morphology), the Domínguez-Sánchez and Walmsley numbers suggest a few hundred to low thousands of confidently labeled real lens/non-lens examples should be enough to safely unfreeze at least the top blocks, consistent with Zoobot's own progressive-unfreezing protocol.

### Gaps
- The "16 examples per class" vs. "400+ examples per class" figures came from a secondary web aggregation (catalyzex/coursework summary) rather than a primary peer-reviewed paper identified with full citation; this specific numeric claim should be treated as indicative, not rigorously sourced, until the original study is located.
- No paper was found that specifically reports the minimum number of *real* labeled lens/non-lens images needed to safely fine-tune deep layers of a lens-finding CNN (as opposed to galaxy-morphology tasks generally) — this is a specific gap for the lens-detection use case.

## Key Question 3: For gravitational lens finding specifically, which papers used transfer learning from a pretrained backbone, what fine-tuning depth/strategy did they use, what performance did they achieve, and did they fine-tune on simulations, real data, or both?

### Takeaway
Most of the classic lens-finding CNN papers (CMU DeepLens, Jacobs et al., Petrillo et al., Canameras et al.) trained their networks **from scratch** on simulated lenses (not transfer learning from ImageNet or another pretrained backbone); genuine transfer learning from a pretrained backbone for lens finding appears concentrated in the newer Euclid Q1 machine-learning pipeline, where the Zoobot galaxy-morphology foundation model is fine-tuned by freezing the EfficientNetB0 backbone and training only a new head.

### Cited Findings
- Lanusse, Ma, Li, Collett, Li, Ravanbakhsh, Mandelbaum & Póczos (2018), "CMU DeepLens: Deep Learning For Automatic Image-based Galaxy-Galaxy Strong Lens Finding", MNRAS / arXiv:1703.02642 — uses a ResNet-style architecture (46 layers deep, pre-activated bottleneck residual units) whose weights are **randomly initialized** following He et al. (2015) and trained **from scratch** (not transfer learning) on 20,000 LSST-like mock/simulated observations. — [arXiv:1703.02642](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.02642)
- CMU DeepLens achieves, for a 99% rejection rate of non-lenses, 90% completeness for lenses with Einstein radii > 1.4″ and S/N > 20 on individual g-band LSST exposures (reported on simulated/mock test data). — [arXiv:1703.02642](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.02642)
- Jacobs, Glazebrook, Collett, More & McCarthy (2017), "Finding strong lenses in CFHTLS using convolutional neural networks", MNRAS / arXiv:1704.02744 — an ensemble of four CNNs trained on simulated galaxy-galaxy lenses (62,406 simulated lenses, 64,673 non-lens negatives); achieved up to 99.8% accuracy and 94–100% purity/completeness on a 2,000-image simulated test set; applied to 171 deg² of real CFHTLS data, flagging 18,861 candidates, recovering 63 known lenses and identifying 139 other candidates. No pretrained/ImageNet backbone or transfer learning was identified in available summaries — training appears to be from scratch on simulations. — [arXiv:1704.02744](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.02744)
- Jacobs et al. (2019), "Finding high-redshift strong lenses in DES using convolutional neural networks", MNRAS 484(4), 5330–5349 / arXiv:1811.03786 — two ensembles of CNNs trained on 250,000 simulated z>0.8 lenses plus simulated non-lenses **and real sources**; identified 84 new "probably"/"definitely" lens candidates after visual inspection of network outputs on real DES Year 3 imaging. Training mixes simulated lenses with real non-lens/real-source examples, but no explicit pretrained-backbone transfer learning was found in available sources. — [arXiv:1811.03786](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1811.03786)
- Petrillo et al. (2017), "Finding Strong Gravitational Lenses in the Kilo Degree Survey with Convolutional Neural Networks", MNRAS / arXiv:1702.07675 — CNN applied to 21,789 colour-magnitude-selected luminous red galaxies (LRGs) from 255 deg² of KiDS; retrieved 761 strong-lens candidates, correctly re-classified 2 of 3 known lenses in the LRG sample, and down-selected 56 reliable candidates after visual inspection. — [arXiv:1702.07675](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1702.07675)
- Petrillo et al. (2019), "Testing Convolutional Neural Networks for finding strong gravitational lenses in KiDS", MNRAS 482, 807–820 / arXiv:1807.04764 — trained two ConvNets on a dataset combining **real KiDS galaxies with simulated lensed sources** (not purely simulated scenes): one on single r-band images (morphology-driven), one on g-r-i composite images (colour+morphology). On the 21,789-LRG test sample, the single-band ConvNet achieved ~40% purity while recovering 3 of 4 confirmed lenses (75% completeness on the known subsample). No evidence of transfer learning from an ImageNet or other external pretrained backbone was found — training is from a combination of real and simulated data, not from a pretrained network. — [arXiv:1807.04764](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1807.04764)
- Cañameras et al. (2021), "HOLISMOKES II: Identifying galaxy-scale strong gravitational lenses in Pan-STARRS using convolutional neural networks", A&A 644, A163 / arXiv:2004.13048 — uses a two-stage pipeline (a catalog/photometric-feature network plus a CNN on image cutouts); trained on positive examples created by painting simulated lensed COSMOS sources onto real Pan-STARRS cutouts of SDSS luminous red galaxies (see Key Question 4 for injection details). Produced 105,760 candidates with CNN score > 0.5, 12,382 with score > 0.9, and 330 high-quality candidates after visual inspection, recovering 23 previously published lens systems. No pretrained external backbone identified — appears to be trained from scratch with the lens-injection training set. — [arXiv:2004.13048](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13048)
- Cañameras et al. (2021), HOLISMOKES VI, "New galaxy-scale strong lens candidates from the HSC-SSP imaging survey", A&A / arXiv:2107.07829 — a ResNet-18-inspired network (64×64×3 input, 18 layers total: initial 3×3/64-feature conv layer, 8 residual blocks, average pooling, flatten, 16-neuron dense layer, single sigmoid output) applied to all 62.5 million HSC Wide galaxies with i-band Kron radius > 0.8″; found 206 new definite/probable lens candidates, 88 previously-lower-confidence candidates upgraded, and recovered 173 known systems; reports false-positive rates as low as ~0.01%. Trained using the HOLISMOKES "painted arcs on real HSC cutouts" simulation approach (balanced training sets of 40,000 positive / 40,000 negative examples, 10″×10″ cutouts) rather than transfer learning from an external pretrained network. — [arXiv:2107.07829](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07829)
- Euclid Collaboration, "Euclid Quick Data Release (Q1). The Strong Lensing Discovery Engine C: Finding lenses with machine learning" (2025), arXiv:2503.15326 — this is the clearest confirmed case of genuine transfer learning from a pretrained backbone for lens finding: Zoobot (EfficientNetB0 backbone, pretrained on ~100 million Galaxy Zoo classifications) was fine-tuned by removing its original head, **freezing** all backbone weights, and training only a new head (first dense layer: 128 units, dropout p=0.2, ReLU; second/output layer: 2 units, softmax) for binary lens/non-lens classification. Of 20 ML models benchmarked on Euclid ERO data, the Zoobot-based model was reported as the best performer; of ~1 million Q1 objects scored, the top-1000-ranked objects contained 122 grade-A and 41 grade-B lenses. The paper notes that further performance gains are possible by varying "the number of layers fine-tuned," implying the frozen-backbone configuration tested was not the only depth explored but is the one reported as best/default. — [arXiv:2503.15326](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.15326)
- The Pearce-Casey et al. (2024) result (as referenced within the Euclid Q1 papers) is the first published application of Zoobot specifically to lens finding, using this frozen-backbone/new-head fine-tuning approach; it is cited as the methodological origin for the Zoobot-based entries in the Euclid Q1 ML comparison. — [arXiv:2503.15326](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.15326)

### Inferences
- There is a clear historical split: the 2017–2021 generation of lens-finding CNNs (CMU DeepLens, Jacobs et al., Petrillo et al., Cañameras et al.) are trained from scratch (or, at most, mix real and simulated images in the training set) rather than using transfer learning from a pretrained backbone in the ULMFiT/Zoobot sense; genuine backbone transfer learning for lens finding is a more recent development tied specifically to the availability of galaxy-morphology foundation models like Zoobot (pretrained ~2021–2022, applied to lens finding from ~2024 onward).
- This means that for a user building on a pretrained backbone (e.g., Zoobot) for lens detection, the closest methodological precedent in the literature is the Euclid Q1 Zoobot pipeline (arXiv:2503.15326) and the companion "from simulations to sky" paper (arXiv:2512.05899, see Key Question 5), not the earlier CMU DeepLens/Jacobs/Petrillo/Cañameras line, which answers a different question (architecture and simulation realism, not transfer learning depth).

### Gaps
- The exact unfreezing depth(s) tested in the Euclid Q1 Zoobot application (beyond "frozen backbone + new head" as the reported configuration) were not found in the available search/fetch results — the paper mentions varying "the number of layers fine-tuned" as a tunable but a full ablation table (if one exists) was not retrieved within the tool budget.
- Whether Jacobs et al. (2017/2019) or Petrillo et al. (2017/2019) used *any* externally pretrained weights (e.g., ImageNet) as initialization, as opposed to random/He initialization, could not be confirmed definitively from secondary sources; direct inspection of each paper's Methods section (not accessed in full due to PDF-fetch limitations) would be needed to rule this out with full certainty.

## Key Question 4: "Lens injection" — papers that inject simulated lensing arcs/sources into real survey images (rather than fully simulated scenes) to close the sim-to-real gap, and their implementation details / quantified improvement

### Takeaway
The HOLISMOKES series (Cañameras et al.) is the clearest, most consistently documented example of "lens injection": simulated lensed-source light (often drawn from the COSMOS catalog) is painted/injected onto real survey cutouts of real foreground deflector galaxies (Pan-STARRS LRGs with SDSS-measured redshift/velocity dispersion, or HSC LRGs), rather than simulating the whole scene; the Euclid Q1 "from simulations to sky" paper (arXiv:2512.05899) independently confirms that adding real lens/non-lens examples (as opposed to, or in addition to, purely synthetic injected training data) is what drives most of the measured real-world performance improvement.

### Cited Findings
- Cañameras et al. (2021), HOLISMOKES II, A&A 644, A163 / arXiv:2004.13048 — positive training examples are created by "painting lensed COSMOS sources on Pan-STARRS image cutouts of lens[ing] luminous red galaxies with known redshift and velocity dispersion from SDSS" — i.e., the foreground deflector galaxy image is real (real Pan-STARRS pixels of a real SDSS-catalogued LRG), while the background lensed source light is synthetic (from the COSMOS source catalog, ray-traced through a lens model using the real galaxy's measured redshift and velocity dispersion) and added onto the real cutout. Full PSF-convolution/noise-model details were not retrievable from the abstract-level fetch available. — [arXiv:2004.13048](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13048)
- Cañameras et al. (2021), HOLISMOKES VI, A&A / arXiv:2107.07829 — same injection philosophy applied to HSC-SSP: "highly-realistic mock simulations" are created by "painting lensed arcs on multiband HSC gri images of [real] galaxies acting as strong lens deflectors," explicitly stated to "account for the quality of HSC imaging and for the presence of artifacts and neighboring galaxies" — i.e., the injection is motivated explicitly as a way to make the simulated positive-class images share the real survey's noise/PSF/artifact characteristics rather than using a fully synthetic scene. Cutouts are 10″×10″; training sets are balanced at 40,000 positive and 40,000 negative examples (and elsewhere in the HOLISMOKES series, 50%/50% balanced sets are standard). — [arXiv:2107.07829](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07829)
- Related augmentation detail found in the lens-injection literature search (source survey covering several lens-finding papers, exact paper not individually confirmed by direct fetch): a lensed source image is first convolved with the local point-spread function (PSF) extracted from the actual corresponding region of the real survey imaging, then this PSF-convolved lensed image is injected into the real survey image centered on the candidate foreground lens galaxy; standard geometric augmentations (random rotations, random x/y shifts, horizontal flips, rescaling by factors between 1/1.1 and 1.1) are then applied on top. This generic recipe matches the HOLISMOKES description qualitatively but could not be tied to one single confirmed arXiv ID within the tool budget — treat as corroborating detail, not an independently confirmed primary citation. — [search aggregation; primary source not individually confirmed](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.07492)
- Euclid Collaboration, "Euclid Quick Data Release (Q1). From simulations to sky: Advancing machine-learning lens detection with real Euclid data" (2025), arXiv:2512.05899, lead author N. E. P. Lines et al. — rather than (or in addition to) synthetic injection, this paper directly tests augmenting the training set with **real** Euclid-discovered lenses and real non-lens/false-positive examples found during the Q1 search. This produces a 25–30% improvement in completeness in terms of expected discoverable-lens yield across Euclid DR1 and the full Euclid Wide Survey, of which roughly 20 percentage points come from including real lenses in training and roughly 5–10 points come from exposure to a more diverse set of real non-lenses/false positives from Q1. — [arXiv:2512.05899](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.05899)
- The same Euclid Q1 paper concludes that the most effective strategy for real-world lens-finder performance is a **combination** of the diversity provided by simulations (including, implicitly, injected-arc-style simulations) with the fidelity provided by real confirmed lenses — i.e., neither pure full-scene simulation, pure lens-injection-on-real-backgrounds, nor pure real-data training alone is reported as optimal; blending is. — [arXiv:2512.05899](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.05899)

### Inferences
- The HOLISMOKES injection recipe (real deflector + synthetic lensed source, built using the real galaxy's own measured physical parameters) is explicitly a middle ground between full scene simulation and the Euclid-style "train partly on real confirmed lenses" approach; it buys realistic noise/PSF/neighbor-contamination characteristics for the "non-lensed-light" part of the image while still allowing arbitrary control over the simulated arc's brightness, size, and lensing geometry for class balance and systematic testing across S/N and Einstein-radius bins.
- Insofar as the Euclid Q1 paper's 92%→50% completeness / 100%→24% purity drop (see Key Question 5) was measured for a simulation-only-trained network, and lens-injection-trained HOLISMOKES networks were not evaluated with the same simulation-vs-real-test protocol in the sources retrieved, it is not possible from the current evidence to state how much of that specific sim-to-real gap lens injection (as opposed to pure full-scene simulation) would close; this is flagged below as a gap rather than inferred.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that **quantifies** the specific domain-gap reduction attributable to the lens-injection technique itself (i.e., a controlled A/B comparison: network trained on full-scene simulations vs. an otherwise-identical network trained on lens-injected-on-real-background images, evaluated on the same real test set). The HOLISMOKES papers describe and use the injection technique and report downstream candidate-recovery numbers, but do not report a side-by-side ablation isolating the injection technique's contribution to closing the sim-to-real gap.
- Metcalf et al. (2019), "The Strong Gravitational Lens Finding Challenge" (arXiv:1802.03609) — confirmed to exist (two simulated datasets, "Space" mimicking Euclid-like and "Ground" mimicking KiDS-like imaging, 20,000 training / 80,000 evaluation images each, methods spanning visual inspection, arc/ring finders, SVMs, and CNNs) — but full text was not directly fetched within the tool budget, so it is **not confirmed** whether this specific challenge paper used or discusses lens injection into real images versus purely simulated scenes; this should be verified by a direct read of arXiv:1802.03609 if more detail is needed.
- Exact noise-model and PSF-convolution implementation details for the HOLISMOKES injection pipeline (precise equations, software, treatment of Poisson noise) could not be extracted because the underlying PDF text was not machine-readable via the available WebFetch tool (binary/compressed stream); only abstract-level and secondary-source descriptions were obtainable within the tool budget.

## Key Question 5: Papers quantifying a sim-to-real accuracy/completeness drop for ML lens finders, and remedies tested

### Takeaway
The clearest, directly quantified example found is the Euclid Q1 "From simulations to sky" paper (arXiv:2512.05899): a network trained purely on simulations recovers up to 92% of simulated lenses at 100% purity, but only 50% completeness at 24% purity on real Euclid sky data — and the paper tests mixing in real confirmed lenses and real false positives as the remedy, reporting a 25–30% completeness-yield improvement from doing so.

### Cited Findings
- Euclid Collaboration (Lines, Collett, Holloway et al., 2025), "Euclid Quick Data Release (Q1). From simulations to sky: Advancing machine-learning lens detection with real Euclid data", arXiv:2512.05899 — explicit quantified sim-to-real drop: a simulation-only-trained lens-finding network "recovers up to 92% of simulated lenses with 100% purity" but "only achieves 50% completeness with 24% purity on real Euclid data" — a roughly 42-percentage-point completeness drop and a roughly 76-percentage-point purity drop moving from the simulated test set to real sky data. — [arXiv:2512.05899](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.05899)
- The same paper's discovery process (500 strong lens candidates identified across 63 deg² combining machine learning, citizen science, and expert visual review) is explicitly what enabled this simulation-vs-real performance gap to be quantified in the first place, since it produced a real-lens test/training set large enough to measure against. — [arXiv:2512.05899](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.05899)
- Remedy tested and quantified: augmenting the training set with real Euclid-confirmed lenses and real non-lens/false-positive examples improves completeness by 25–30% (in terms of expected discoverable-lens yield for Euclid DR1 and the full Euclid Wide Survey) relative to training on simulations alone; of this gain, approximately 20 percentage points are attributed to including real lenses in training, and approximately 5–10 percentage points to exposure to a more diverse set of real non-lenses/false positives encountered during the Q1 search. — [arXiv:2512.05899](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.05899)
- The paper's overall conclusion/remedy: the best-performing real-world strategy combines the class diversity available from simulations with the fidelity/realism available from real confirmed lenses — neither purely simulated training nor (implicitly, given how little real-lens data exists for any given survey) purely real-data training is sufficient alone. — [arXiv:2512.05899](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.05899)

### Inferences
- This 92%/100%→50%/24% result is a close match to the kind of "sim-to-real accuracy drop" the task description anticipated (the "~95% on simulated test data to ~50% on real data" pattern) and should be treated as the primary citable quantitative anchor for that claim in the final report; the specific numbers are 92% (not 95%) completeness at 100% purity on simulated data, dropping to 50% completeness at 24% purity on real Euclid data.
- Given that this is the newest and most directly on-point source found (a 2025 Euclid Q1 companion paper specifically designed to measure and remedy this gap), it is likely the single most important citation for closing Key Question 5 and should anchor any discussion of sim-to-real degradation and remedies in the final report.

### Gaps
- No earlier (pre-2025) paper was found that quantifies an analogous sim-to-real completeness/purity drop for lens finders (e.g., for CMU DeepLens, Jacobs et al., Petrillo et al., or Cañameras et al.); those papers report simulated-test-set performance and/or visually-inspected real-candidate yields, but not a matched simulated-vs-real completeness/purity comparison on the same evaluation protocol. If such an earlier quantification exists, it was not surfaced within the search budget used here.
- It was not established from the sources retrieved whether the Euclid Q1 paper also tested the lens-injection-on-real-backgrounds technique (as distinct from "train on real confirmed lenses") as an intermediate remedy, or whether its "simulations" baseline refers to full-scene simulation specifically rather than some injection-based simulation — this ambiguity about exactly what "simulations" means in this paper's 92%/50% comparison should be flagged to the report writer and, if possible, resolved by a direct full-text read of arXiv:2512.05899 (only partially retrievable here due to PDF size/encoding limits on the WebFetch tool).
