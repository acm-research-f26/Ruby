# Closing Zoobot's sim-to-real lens gap

The 94–98% → 48–61% collapse this project measured going from simulated to real Euclid test images is not an anomaly or a sign of a broken pipeline — it is the same failure mode the Euclid Collaboration itself quantified at far larger scale: a simulation-only lens finder recovered **92% of simulated lenses at 100% purity but only 50% completeness at 24% purity on real Euclid Q1 sky data** ([arXiv:2512.05899](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.05899)). The literature converges on a clear ranking of fixes. Architecture changes to Zoobot itself are not the lever to pull first: Zoobot's ConvNeXt/EfficientNet backbone, fine-tuned with a documented partial-unfreezing recipe, was already the **best of five competing ML lens-finders** in the Euclid Q1 benchmark ([arXiv:2503.15326](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.15326)), so the project's gap is almost certainly a data-distribution problem, not a model-capacity problem. The two most literature-validated remedies are (a) mixing a modest number of real lens/non-lens examples into training — which closed **25–30 percentage points** of completeness in the Euclid study — and (b) formal domain adaptation (DANN/ADDA/MMD/optimal transport), which has produced **9–41 percentage-point** gains on structurally identical sim-to-real astronomy classification tasks ([arXiv:2410.01203](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01203v1); [arXiv:2511.18590](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.18590v1)). A generalist-plus-compound-lens-specialist cascade, by contrast, is architecturally appealing but has no published precedent and carries a specific, well-documented failure mode — a hard stage-1 gate silently caps the whole pipeline's recall on exactly the rare class the specialist is meant to rescue. Given this project's extreme data scarcity (23 real test images, presumably even fewer real positives), the most tractable, literature-backed path is: keep Zoobot's architecture, fine-tune with LP-FT-style partial unfreezing, inject whatever real lens/non-lens examples exist directly into the training mix, and layer in domain adaptation or self-supervised pretraining on unlabeled real Euclid cutouts — in that order of effort versus expected payoff.

## Zoobot already beat four bespoke lens-finders, but its failures cluster exactly where thin arcs live

Zoobot is not a lensing model wearing a disguise — it is a general-purpose galaxy-morphology foundation model, trained as a multi-headed classifier to predict the entire Galaxy Zoo decision tree across **~820,000 images and 100 million+ volunteer votes** spanning GZ2, GZ Hubble, GZ CANDELS, GZ DECaLS/DESI, and GZ Cosmic Dawn ([Zoobot docs](https://zoobot.readthedocs.io/en/latest/pretrained_models.html)). It ships four backbone families — ConvNeXt, MaxViT, ResNet, EfficientNet — with ConvNeXt-Nano (15.6M parameters) recommended as the practical default and ConvNeXt-Base/MaxViT-Base reserved for maximal accuracy on larger GPU budgets ([Zoobot docs](https://zoobot.readthedocs.io/en/latest/pretrained_models.html)). Critically, Zoobot's own scaling-law paper (Walmsley et al. 2024, 20 co-authors) found that scaling pretraining data and parameter count alone is **not sufficient to close a domain gap** for visually distinct downstream tasks, and explicitly recommends "in-domain adaptation followed by targeted downstream labelling" instead ([arXiv:2404.02973](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02973)) — a direct, pre-existing warning against expecting a bigger or better-pretrained Zoobot checkpoint to solve a sim-to-real problem on its own.

When the Euclid Collaboration actually applied Zoobot to lens finding (Lines, Collett, Walmsley et al., "Strong Lensing Discovery Engine C," [arXiv:2503.15326](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.15326)), it benchmarked five independent networks on ~1 million Q1 objects and found Zoobot's fine-tuned ConvNeXt-Nano **outperformed all four bespoke/from-scratch networks** — 122 grade-A and 41 grade-B lenses in its top-1000 ranked objects, versus 69 A / 34 B for the next-best model (a simple four-layer CNN) and single digits for the rest. The authors attribute this advantage to three factors directly relevant to this project's situation: transfer learning needs **~10³ labeled examples versus 10⁴–10⁵** for training from scratch, it overfits less because the backbone already encodes generic morphology, and it avoids the shortcut learning (e.g., PSF-orientation artifacts) that from-scratch networks trained purely on simulations can latch onto. But Zoobot's errors were not random: its top-100 false positives were dominated by chance alignments of unrelated galaxies (not image artifacts), edge-on disc lenses ranked poorly because they look visually atypical, and of four known double-source-plane lenses, two ranked in the top 10 while two others ranked **1902nd and 2349th** — specifically the ones with smaller Einstein radii, fainter arcs, and lens-light blending. Purity collapsed from 35% (pure subsample) to 20% once completeness reached 60%, and reaching 100% completeness required inspecting the top 10% of the entire dataset. No paper traces this to a specific architectural mechanism (e.g., ConvNeXt's patchify stem or downsampling path) — it is reported only as an empirical pattern, so "fix the backbone's receptive field" is not a literature-supported intervention; "get it more and better-targeted examples of exactly these failure modes" is. Tellingly, the simplest competing model (the four-layer CNN) ranked all four DSPLs in the top 500 — substantially better than Zoobot on that one rare subclass — which the Euclid team reads as evidence that different architectures capture complementary information, a point that bears directly on this project's ensemble question below.

## Partial unfreezing plus real lens injection is the field's actual playbook, not a hypothetical

The transfer-learning literature gives a specific, reproducible recipe, and Zoobot's own team already executed an astronomy-specific version of it. General ML foundations — Yosinski et al.'s finding that early CNN layers encode generic features while later layers specialize ([arXiv:1411.1792](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1411.1792)), and ULMFiT's gradual unfreezing with discriminative (smaller) learning rates on earlier layers ([arXiv:1801.06146](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.06146v2)) — translate directly into Walmsley et al.'s own ring-galaxy fine-tuning protocol: freeze the backbone, train a new head alone first, then progressively unfreeze from the top across six discrete depths at a learning rate **100x lower** than head-training, with batch-norm layers never unfrozen ([arXiv:2110.12735](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.12735)). For the Euclid Q1 lens application specifically, the team found an optimum at unfreezing only the classification head plus the ConvNeXt block(s) immediately preceding it — too little unfreezing leaves the classifier stuck resembling its original non-lensing task, too much "erases more of the useful features" ([arXiv:2503.15326](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.15326)). A second, directly relevant finding from Kumar et al. (ICLR 2022) is that **naive full fine-tuning can actively hurt out-of-distribution performance**: across 10 distribution-shift benchmarks, full fine-tuning gained ~2 points in-distribution but lost ~7 points out-of-distribution relative to a disciplined "linear-probe-then-fine-tune" (LP-FT) schedule, because jointly updating the head and backbone distorts the pretrained low-level features the model needs for the shifted domain ([arXiv:2202.10054](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.10054v1)). This maps almost exactly onto this project's situation: training on simulated lenses and fully fine-tuning the whole network risks baking in simulation-specific shortcuts precisely because the sim and real distributions differ, so an LP-FT-style approach — train the head frozen first, then carefully unfreeze the last block(s) at a low learning rate — is the literature-prescribed hedge against exactly this symptom.

How much labeled data is "enough" to unfreeze more? The evidence converges on a rule of thumb: tens of labels support only a frozen head/linear probe; a few hundred support partial unfreezing; thousands are needed for full fine-tuning to be safe. Domínguez-Sánchez et al. found that transfer learning cut the labels needed for cross-survey morphology adaptation from ~5,000 (training from scratch) to **300–500** while still reaching >95% accuracy ([arXiv:1807.00807](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1807.00807)), and Walmsley's ring-galaxy results show the galaxy-pretrained backbone "vastly outperforms" alternatives in the 10–100 label regime specifically. With this project's ~6,000 simulated images but only 23 real test images (and presumably far fewer confirmed real positives), the safe interpretation is that the real-label budget is almost certainly in the "head-only or shallow-unfreeze" regime, not the "unfreeze everything" regime — reinforcing that discipline about freezing depth, not just more simulated training data, is the lever most likely to help.

On the training-data side, "lens injection" is the field's established workaround for exactly this problem, and it predates Zoobot. The HOLISMOKES series (Cañameras et al.) builds positive training examples by painting simulated, ray-traced COSMOS source light onto **real** survey cutouts of real foreground deflector galaxies with known SDSS-measured redshift and velocity dispersion — for Pan-STARRS ([arXiv:2004.13048](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13048)) and later HSC-SSP ([arXiv:2107.07829](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07829)) — explicitly so the "non-arc" part of every training image already carries the real survey's noise, PSF, and neighbor-contamination statistics, while the arc itself remains fully controllable for class balance and S/N/Einstein-radius coverage. No paper in this search isolates, in a controlled A/B test, how much of the sim-to-real gap lens injection alone closes versus full-scene simulation — a genuine open gap this project could fill empirically — but the companion Euclid Q1 paper on exactly this problem (Lines et al., "From simulations to sky," [arXiv:2512.05899](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.05899)) found that augmenting training with **real confirmed lenses and real non-lens false positives** drove a 25–30% completeness-yield improvement, with roughly 20 points attributable to the real lenses and 5–10 points to more diverse real negatives. Their explicit conclusion is that neither pure simulation nor pure real-data training is optimal — the best strategy blends simulated diversity with real-data fidelity. For this project, that argues for injecting real Euclid cutouts (even the 23 held-out test images, split and reused carefully, or any other real Euclid negatives that can be gathered) into training rather than reserving all real data purely for evaluation.

## A generalist-to-specialist cascade is unproven and risks capping recall before the specialist ever sees the hard cases

No paper surveyed describes the exact architecture this project is considering — a stage-1 generalist lens/non-lens classifier (Zoobot) that filters or routes "leftover" ambiguous or compound-lens candidates to a dedicated stage-2 specialist. Wilde et al. (2022) establish the underlying motivation clearly: CNNs trained for general lens finding show sharply degraded recall on rare configurations — **75% on compound arcs and 56% on double rings** for their best model (OU-200), dropping to 63% and 47% for a smaller model (OU-66) — versus much higher recall on ordinary single lenses ([arXiv:2202.12776](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2202.12776)). But their own attempt at ensembling seven models via t-SNE fusion ("OU-T-SNE") actually *underperformed* the single best model (F1 0.9423 vs. 0.9687), and their stated future-work recommendation is generic ("attention must be paid to training... to accommodate anticipated interesting subsets"), not a concrete cascade design. Existing multi-stage lens-finding pipelines that do exist — HOLISMOKES-II's cheap catalog filter feeding an expensive image CNN ([arXiv:2004.13048](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.13048)), and AgileLens's iterative self-retraining loop ([arXiv:2604.06648](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.06648)) — stage by **computational cost**, not by **rarity of configuration**, so they are not direct precedent for a generalist/compound-lens-specialist split. The Euclid Discovery Engine's own solution to rare-configuration coverage was not a specialist cascade but an **ensemble of eight independently-trained generalist networks** from five teams, combined with citizen-science review, reaching 52% purity / 50% completeness overall against a 0.05% random baseline ([arXiv:2503.15328](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.15328)) — reinforcing that "run several different generalist models and combine their outputs" has published precedent and measurable benefit, while "train a dedicated specialist gated on a generalist's output" does not yet.

The general cascade-detection literature explains why this matters. From Viola-Jones onward, the textbook-acknowledged core limitation of any cascade is that **a rejection at an early stage is unrecoverable by any later stage** — "whatever stage 1 misses, stage 2 never sees" ([Bourdev & Brandt, CVPR 2005](https://mlanthology.org/cvpr/2005/bourdev2005cvpr-robust)); modern retrieval-reranking literature calls this the "recall ceiling," warning that pipeline health looks fine on the metrics people watch while true positives are silently dropped before the second stage ever runs ([arXiv:2609.27953](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.27953)). Combined with Wilde et al.'s measured 52–76% generalist recall on exactly the rare configurations a specialist would be built to rescue, a hard stage-1 gate would cap overall compound-lens recall at whatever Zoobot's own recall on those configurations already is — the specialist could never see the compound lenses Zoobot filters out before it runs. The more literature-aligned implementation, if this project pursues a cascade, borrows from modern long-tail/mixture-of-experts work rather than classical cascades: Kang et al. (ICLR 2020) show that decoupling representation learning from classification — i.e., keep the shared backbone, adjust only the decision layer for the tail class — beats re-training the whole network on imbalanced data ([arXiv:1910.09217](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.09217)), and RIDE (ICLR 2021) shows a diversity-promoting loss across multiple expert heads, with inference-time routing based on *input ambiguity* rather than a hard class decision, gains 5–7% on long-tailed benchmarks specifically by avoiding early hard rejection of hard/rare cases ([arXiv:2010.01809](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.01809)). Practically: route on Zoobot's *uncertainty*, not its top-1 decision, and run the compound-lens specialist on a generously permissive band of ambiguous-to-positive scores rather than only on cases Zoobot confidently flags — otherwise the cascade degenerates into "Zoobot alone, plus latency." No literature was found addressing a gate trained with only a handful of real positive examples (this project's likely regime is even more extreme than the ImageNet-LT/iNaturalist tail-class benchmarks RIDE and Kang et al. use), so this remains a genuinely open experimental question rather than a solved one.

## Domain adaptation and real-data mixing are the best-evidenced fixes; GAN translation and domain randomization are promising but thin

Four technique families address sim-to-real generalization directly, and the evidence base differs sharply in maturity across them. **Domain adaptation** (DANN, MMD, ADDA, WDGRL, optimal transport) is the most mature and best quantified, with results specifically on lens finding and closely analogous astronomy tasks:

| Study | Task | Without adaptation | With adaptation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Parul et al. ([2410.01203](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01203v1)) | Sim-to-real lens finding (HSC) | AUROC 0.921; TPR@1%FPR 0.354 | AUROC 0.940–0.942; TPR@1%FPR 0.528 (WDGRL) |
| DeepMerge II ([2103.01373](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.01373)) | Sim-to-real merger classification | 50% accuracy | 69% accuracy (MMD + transfer) |
| DeepAdversaries ([2112.14299](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2112.14299)) | Low-noise-sim to noisy-sim morphology | 43% accuracy (ResNet18) | 66% accuracy (MMD) |
| Sim-to-real morphology ([2511.18590](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.18590v1)) | TNG50-sim to real SDSS, 3-class | ~46% macro-F1 | ~87% macro-F1 (optimal transport) |
| DeepAstroUDA ([2211.00677](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.00677)) | Cross-survey/depth morphology | F1 0.32±0.31 | F1 0.73±0.13 |

These gains — **9 to 41 percentage points** depending on task and metric — are not marginal; several are larger than the gap this project needs to close. Critically, unsupervised DA (no real labels) consistently underperforms supervised DA/direct fine-tuning on real labels where both are tested (Parul et al.: 0.940 unsupervised vs. 0.987 supervised baseline, only reaching 0.991 with supervised DA), meaning DA is best understood as a way to extract more value from whatever small amount of real labeled data exists, not a substitute for having any. No study was found applying DANN/MMD/ADDA specifically to a Zoobot-architecture backbone — all cited examples use ResNet, plain CNN, or equivariant NN encoders — so transfer of these exact gains to a ConvNeXt-based Zoobot fine-tune is an extrapolation, not a confirmed result, but the mechanism (aligning feature distributions between sim and real domains) is architecture-agnostic and should transfer in principle.

**Self-supervised pretraining** on unlabeled real survey images is the second-most mature path and architecturally the closest fit to a Zoobot pipeline, since Zoobot already exists as a fine-tuning backbone. Hayat et al. and Stein et al. show contrastive pretraining on real SDSS/DESI imagery (Stein et al.'s corpus: tens of millions of real galaxy images) cuts the labeled-data requirement by **2–4x** and enables lens-candidate discovery via similarity search in representation space ([arXiv:2012.13083](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2012.13083); [arXiv:2110.13151](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.13151)). Walmsley's own hybrid contrastive+supervised pretraining work (BYOL-based, on GZ-Evo) is explicitly motivated by the mismatch between abundant unlabeled Euclid imaging and scarce human labels and was reportedly deployed to measure morphology across Euclid data as of mid-2024 ([arXiv:2206.11927](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2206.11927)) — this is the most directly applicable existing precedent for this project's Zoobot pipeline specifically, even though no source combines it with lens-finding fine-tuning explicitly. The clearest lens-specific self-supervised result is LenSiam, which pretrained with lensing-specific augmentations on simulated images and beat both generic self-supervised and ImageNet-supervised pretraining on Einstein-radius regression (R² 0.586 vs. 0.426 vs. 0.360) ([arXiv:2311.10100](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2311.10100)) — demonstrating that domain-specific augmentation choices matter more than the pretraining paradigm per se, a finding this project could apply even without new real data, by self-supervised-pretraining on unlabeled real Euclid cutouts (which likely exist in much greater quantity than the 23 labeled test images) before fine-tuning for lens classification.

**GAN-based image translation** and **domain randomization** are both plausible but empirically thin for this specific task. The one dedicated lens-finding paper combining GANs with simulation (Sheng et al., AISTATS 2022) reports an "order of magnitude" improvement over naive sim-only training by using adversarial networks to suppress the simulation artifacts a classifier would otherwise key on as spurious lens-identifying shortcuts ([arXiv:2210.11681](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.11681)) — directionally strong, but reported only as an aggregate pipeline result, not an isolated GAN-only ablation, and no one has tested a clean CycleGAN sim→real-appearance translation step before classifier training in this domain despite that exact technique working for PSF/deconvolution learning elsewhere in astronomy. Domain randomization (Tobin et al., robotics, [arXiv:1703.06907](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1703.06907)) has no dedicated astronomy citation at all — lens-finding pipelines already randomize noise/PSF/background as routine augmentation, but no paper frames or validates this deliberately as domain randomization in Tobin's sense (training on an intentionally over-widened, non-realistic parameter range to force domain-invariant features). This is a real literature gap rather than a dead end: it is a nearly free ablation for this project to run (deliberately widen the simulated noise/PSF/background ranges beyond best estimates of the real Euclid distribution) since it requires no real data and no architecture change, but any expected benefit is extrapolated from robotics, not demonstrated in this domain.

Given this project's specific constraints — roughly 6,000 simulated training images, only 23 real test images, and (implicitly) very few if any labeled real positives — the ranking that follows from the evidence is: **real-data mixing/lens injection first** (cheapest, most directly evidenced at the exact scale of "add what little real data you have," 25–30 point gains in the closest analog study), **then supervised or semi-supervised domain adaptation** (9–41 point gains elsewhere, but untested specifically on a ConvNeXt/Zoobot backbone), **then self-supervised pretraining on unlabeled real Euclid imagery** if more unlabeled real cutouts can be gathered beyond the 23 labeled test images, and **domain randomization as a free, parallel ablation** regardless of which other path is chosen. GAN-based translation and a generalist/specialist cascade are worth tracking but should be treated as exploratory, not load-bearing, given how thin or absent the direct evidence is.

## Conclusion

The pattern across every source in this research is the same: the sim-to-real gap in lens finding is a property of the *problem*, not a defect specific to this project's implementation or to Zoobot as a backbone — Zoobot already outperformed four bespoke networks in the one head-to-head benchmark that exists, and the field's own flagship paper on this exact failure mode reports a comparably severe collapse (92%/100% simulated to 50%/24% real). That reframes the task: rather than redesigning Zoobot's architecture or inventing a novel cascade with no precedent, the highest-leverage moves are procedural and data-centric — discipline about which layers get unfrozen and at what learning rate (LP-FT-style, following Zoobot's own published ring-galaxy and Euclid lens-finding protocols), real lens/non-lens examples injected directly into the training mix rather than reserved only for evaluation, and one of the domain-adaptation or self-supervised techniques that have already shown double-digit percentage-point gains on structurally identical astronomy tasks. The compound-lens specialist cascade is the one idea in this project's plan without published support, and the literature gives a specific, actionable reason for caution: a hard stage-1 gate will cap the whole pipeline's recall at whatever Zoobot's own recall on compound/rare configurations already is, silently discarding exactly the cases the specialist exists to catch — so if pursued, it should route on uncertainty rather than a hard threshold, and the absence of prior art here is itself a genuine opportunity to publish a first result rather than a sign the idea is unworkable.
